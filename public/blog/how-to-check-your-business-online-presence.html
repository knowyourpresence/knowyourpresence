How to Check Your Business's Online Presence (Free)

5 min read  ·  October 2026

Last month I was talking to a café owner in Brisbane who'd been in business for six years. Good coffee, loyal regulars, great location. But she was baffled — a competitor two streets away had opened 18 months ago and was already busier on weekdays.

I pulled up both businesses on my phone. Her competitor had 140 Google reviews averaging 4.7 stars, a website that loaded in under two seconds, and posted on Instagram three times a week. Her café had 11 reviews, a website built in 2019 that barely worked on mobile, and hadn't posted on social media since March.

That's the gap. Not the coffee. The online presence.

Here's how to honestly check where your business stands — without spending anything.

Start with Google, because that's where your customers start

Open Google and search for your own business name. Not what you offer — your actual business name. What do you see?

If a panel appears on the right side of the screen (or at the top on mobile) showing your address, hours, photos, and reviews — that's your Google Business Profile. If nothing appears, you either don't have one or it's not verified. That's a serious problem and the first thing to fix.

Assuming you have one, look at it honestly:

  • Are your hours correct right now? (Not just usually — are they correct today, including any holiday hours?)
  • Do you have at least 10 photos? Businesses with more than 10 photos get dramatically more direction requests than those with 1 or 2 stock images.
  • Is your business description actually describing what you do, or is it vague filler?
  • When did someone last ask a question via Q&A, and did you answer it?

Now search for what you offer in your area — "electrician Melbourne" or "hair salon Bondi" or whatever your trade is. Do you appear in the top 3 results? If you're not in the map pack, most people never find you through Google search at all.

Your reviews are doing more work than you think

People read reviews the way they used to ask a friend. And they're not just looking at the star rating — they're reading how you responded to the bad ones.

Check your Google reviews right now. How many do you have? What's your average? More importantly — have you replied to all of them, including the negative ones?

A business with 4.2 stars and 80 reviews will usually outperform one with 4.8 stars and 6 reviews. Volume signals that you've actually served a lot of people. Six reviews, even perfect ones, just looks like your mum and your mates.

While you're at it, search for your business on Facebook too. Facebook reviews still matter for service businesses, especially in suburban and regional Australia. And check if you appear on TrueLocal or ProductReview.com.au — some industries (trades, home services) get a lot of traffic through those.

Look at your social media like a stranger would

Open your Facebook business page or Instagram on a browser you're not logged into, or ask someone who doesn't know you to look it up. What's their first impression?

The things that kill credibility fast: a profile photo that's blurry or obviously a logo from 2014, a cover photo that's still the default grey, a bio that doesn't say where you are or what you do, and the last post being from eight months ago.

That last one matters more than people realise. An inactive page doesn't just look unprofessional — it makes people wonder if you're still open. I've seen businesses lose sales because a potential customer checked their Facebook, saw nothing posted in four months, and assumed they'd closed down.

You don't need to post every day. Once or twice a week, consistently, is enough to signal that you're active and care.

Test your website like someone who's never heard of you

Pull up your website on your phone — not your laptop. More than 70% of local business searches in Australia happen on mobile, so if your site is hard to use on a phone, that's where most of your visitors are bouncing.

Time how long it takes to load. If it's more than three seconds, you're losing people. Attention spans are short and options are plentiful — nobody waits for a slow local business website.

Then ask yourself: within five seconds of landing on the page, does a new visitor know what you do, where you are, and how to contact you? If they have to scroll or click around to find your phone number, that's a problem.

Go to pagespeed.web.dev and enter your URL. It's free and takes 30 seconds. Anything below 50 on mobile is worth fixing.

Compare yourself to who's actually beating you

Search Google for your core service in your suburb or city. Look at the top 3 results that aren't ads. Those are the businesses taking the customers you should be getting.

Compare their review count and rating to yours. Look at their website. Check if they're active on social. You're not trying to copy them — you're trying to understand the gap.

Most small business owners are surprised when they do this properly. The businesses ranking above them aren't necessarily better at the actual work — they've just invested more time in being findable.

What to do once you know where you stand

Don't try to fix everything at once. Pick the highest-impact thing first and do it properly.

For most businesses, that's Google. Getting your Business Profile complete and verified, then starting to actively ask happy customers for reviews, will have more impact on your local visibility than almost anything else you can do.

After that, it's reviews. Then your website speed and mobile experience. Then social consistency. In that order, for most small businesses.

The café owner in Brisbane I mentioned at the start? She spent four weekends working through those priorities. Six months later she had 67 Google reviews, a new website, and was posting on Instagram regularly. Her Tuesday mornings — previously her quietest time — were now reliably busy.

Nothing changed about her coffee or her service. Just how visible and trustworthy she looked to people searching online.
