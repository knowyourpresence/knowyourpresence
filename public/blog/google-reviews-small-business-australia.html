Google Reviews for Small Business: The Australian Guide

7 min read  ·  October 2026

A plumber in Sydney told me something that stuck with me. He said: "I used to think the best advertising was word of mouth. Now I realise word of mouth has moved online and I was the last to know."

He's right. Google reviews are word of mouth at scale. A recommendation from one happy customer used to travel to maybe five or ten people in their circle. The same customer leaving a Google review reaches every person who searches for your service in your area — forever.

This guide covers everything you actually need to know about Google reviews for a small business in Australia: how to get more of them, how to handle the bad ones, and how to use them to win more customers.

Why Google reviews matter more than any other platform

Australia has a lot of review platforms — Facebook, TrueLocal, ProductReview.com.au, Yelp, Zomato for food. All of them have some value. But Google reviews are in a different category, for one simple reason: they appear directly in search results.

When someone searches for your business name or your service in your area, your Google reviews are the first thing they see — before your website, before your social media, before anything else. Your star rating is sitting right there in the search results, deciding whether someone clicks through or keeps scrolling.

On top of that, Google uses reviews as a ranking signal. More reviews, higher average rating, and recent review activity all contribute to where you appear in local search. Two businesses offering the same service in the same suburb — the one with 60 reviews will almost always rank above the one with 8, all else being equal.

How many reviews do you actually need?

The honest answer is: more than your nearest competitor. But that's a moving target, so here's a more useful framework.

Under 10 reviews: you have a credibility problem. Most people who land on your listing will feel uncertain. This is your urgent priority.

10–30 reviews: you're visible but not convincing. For lower-value purchases, this might be fine. For anything where trust matters — trades, health, legal, finance — people want more social proof before committing.

30–80 reviews: you're competitive. This is where most established small businesses should aim to be.

80+ reviews: you're a category leader online. At this volume, your rating starts to feel like a genuine consensus rather than a sample.

One thing most business owners don't realise: Google prominently displays "X reviews in the last year" on your profile. A business with 60 reviews total but only 3 in the past year looks stagnant. Fresh reviews signal an active, currently-trading business.

The right way to ask for reviews

The single most effective thing you can do to get more Google reviews is simply ask — quickly, after a positive experience, with a direct link that makes it effortless.

Timing is everything. The window is usually within 24 hours of the job being done, the appointment finishing, or the product being delivered. After that, the momentum fades and the likelihood of them actually leaving a review drops sharply.

Here's what works for most small businesses:

  • Text message — the most effective channel for trades and service businesses. Send it the same day. Keep it short and personal.
  • Email — works well for professional services and retail. Slightly lower conversion than text, but scales better if you have an email list.
  • In person — genuinely works, especially in hospitality or health. Saying "If you're happy with today, a Google review would really help us out" is not awkward — customers who like you want to help.
  • QR code on your receipt, invoice, or business card — passive but accumulates over time.

The most important thing: give them a direct link to your review page. Don't just tell them to "search for us on Google." Every extra step you add reduces completion by a significant margin.

To get your link, search for your business on Google, click on your profile, click "Get more reviews," and copy the link it gives you. That's what to send.

What the message should look like

Text message example

"Hi [Name], thanks for having us in today. If you're happy with the work, a quick Google review would mean a lot to us — it only takes a minute: [your review link]. Thanks again, [Your name]"

That's it. No elaborate script. No asking them to mention specific things. Just genuine, human, easy.

What you shouldn't do: offer incentives for reviews. Google's terms explicitly prohibit this, and it can get your reviews removed or your listing penalised. It also doesn't work — reviews that people leave for a discount often read like they left a review for a discount.

How to respond to reviews — especially bad ones

Most business owners respond to positive reviews with a cheerful "Thanks so much!" and then panic when a negative one arrives. Here's a different way to think about it.

Positive reviews: respond briefly and genuinely. Vary your responses — don't copy-paste the same reply. Mention something specific from their review. It takes 30 seconds and shows that a real person is reading and caring.

Negative reviews: this is actually where you can win.

Research consistently shows that how a business responds to a bad review matters more than the review itself. A thoughtful, professional response to a 1-star review can reassure potential customers more than ten 5-star reviews. It shows you take problems seriously, you're accountable, and you're not the kind of business that goes silent or gets defensive when something goes wrong.

The response to a bad review isn't for the person who left it. It's for every potential customer who reads it later. Write it for them.

How to respond to a negative review

Keep it short. Acknowledge what they said without being dismissive. Apologise for the experience — not necessarily for being wrong, but for the fact that they had a poor experience. Invite them to contact you directly to resolve it. Don't argue, don't over-explain, and don't make excuses.

Negative review response example

"Hi [Name], thanks for letting us know. I'm sorry to hear your experience wasn't what we'd hope for — that's genuinely not the standard we aim for. Please reach out to us directly at [email/phone] so we can make it right. — [Your name]"

What you should not do: respond angrily, identify who the customer is publicly, dispute their version of events in your response, or ignore negative reviews entirely. All of these make things worse.

Can you get fake reviews removed?

Sometimes. Google has a reporting function for reviews that violate their policies — fake reviews, reviews from people who were never your customer, reviews from competitors, or reviews that contain spam or inappropriate content.

To report a review: go to your Google Business Profile, find the review, click the three dots next to it, and select "Report review." Google will investigate but doesn't guarantee removal, and the process can take weeks.

For persistent cases, you can escalate through Google's Business Profile support. For reviews that are clearly defamatory, Australian defamation law may apply — legal advice would be worth getting if it's causing serious harm to your business.

The practical reality is that removal is slow and uncertain. The faster fix is usually to generate enough legitimate positive reviews that the fake or unfair one gets buried in the overall sample.

Your reviews are part of your larger online presence

Reviews are one piece of the picture. But they work in combination with everything else — your Google Business Profile completeness, your website quality, your social media activity. A business with 80 reviews but a slow website and an incomplete profile is still leaving customers on the table.

The compound effect of reviews

The thing about reviews that makes them worth serious attention is that the benefit compounds. A business that consistently gets 3–4 new reviews a month will have 36–48 new reviews in a year. Over three years, that's over 100 reviews — and by that point, the review count itself becomes a competitive moat that's genuinely hard for a newer business to overcome.

It just requires building the habit now. Ask every satisfied customer. Make it easy. Respond to every review, good or bad. Don't let weeks pass without fresh activity on your profile.

The plumber from Sydney I mentioned at the start? He went from 9 reviews to 74 in about eight months — just by sending a follow-up text to every job he completed. His Google ranking improved, his inbound calls increased, and he stopped relying on an expensive advertising subscription he'd been paying for years.

Word of mouth hasn't died. It just lives on Google now.
